UENO BANK: the bank that brought down a president

The collapse of Paraguay's bank with the most customers was not just a financial failure. It showed what happens when a bank, the body meant to supervise it and political power are connected, and in four days it cost Santiago Peña the presidency.

By Javier Salazar Segales November 13, 2026
Latin America UENO BANK: the bank that brought down a president Financial markets / International economics

On the night of 2 November, the board of the Central Bank of Paraguay (BCP) voted unanimously to begin the resolution of UENO BANK, after confirming that its solvency ratio had fallen to less than half the legal minimum. The announcement came in the early hours of the 3rd and suspended the app, the cards and the accounts of some 2.9 million customers, around 60% of the country's adult population. Four days later, the Senate had removed President Santiago Peña, who was placed under house arrest, and Vice President Pedro Alliana had already been sworn in. Yet more than the speed of the outcome, what matters is the question it leaves: how could a bank with such serious problems grow so much, so quickly and with so much protection?

The group's origins are far removed from the image of a digital bank. In 1984, Víctor Hugo Vázquez founded Credicentro, a small lending house in Mercado 4 in Asunción, the capital's great popular market, in the same premises where his mother had run a shop since 1956. It is remarkable that, in less than a generation, that credit counter became the bank with the most customers in the country.

UENO's story sums up a decade of fintech in Latin America. In 2021, after the BCP blocked a first merger attempt, Grupo Vázquez bought 65% of Financiera El Comercio, the country's oldest finance company, and used it to absorb its own Credicentro, in which Santiago Peña had been a shareholder since 2019. In 2022 it became the UENO brand, in 2023 it obtained a banking licence and in 2024 it absorbed Visión Banco. By the end of 2025 it held 8.9% of the system's deposits and was the country's leading institution by number of customers. In March 2026 it placed USD 350 million in international bonds, the largest issue ever by a Paraguayan bank, and had brought in the Hungarian bank OTP as a minority shareholder. It is important to stress that the financial inclusion was real: according to the bank itself, more than a million people opened their first account there. That is precisely why the collapse had an unprecedented social impact in the country.

That growth had a very visible engine: cashback. The terms and conditions published by the bank itself show that from 2024 UENO refunded 50% of the first purchase made with its credit card, a promotion renewed again and again until at least August 2026, and that campaigns such as “Día u” on 5 November 2025 extended that 50% to customers of every tier. During the 2024 “UENO black shopping week” it refunded half of all purchases by new customers. In 2025 it promised to refund 100% of spending on the Albirroja card if the national team won its two matches, and it went as far as offering discounts of up to 40% on fuel. According to testimonies gathered by Argentina's La Nación, days before the intervention the bank was still offering 50% cashback to those who kept 10 million guaraníes locked in. The current president of the BCP, Jorge Schreiner, admitted that the regulator was wrong to allow a bank with “crazy promotions” and claimed that he had predicted its fall as early as 2023. It is important to stress that losing money to win customers is a common strategy among fintechs. Even so, this shows how far UENO's growth was bought with money, and forces us to ask who was really paying for those gifts.

The problem was not the technology but the balance sheet. According to the Public Prosecutor's Office and the BCP, UENO lent excessively to companies in its own controlling group. In June 2026, when the supervisor ordered it to get rid of those loans, it transferred a USD 74.4 million portfolio to Vinanzas S.A., a related company that, according to investigators, was indirectly funded with money from the same bank. This shows a familiar pattern: the risk did not disappear, it simply moved so that it would not show up in the indicators. When the BCP adjusted the financial statements, the bank's real solvency was laid bare.

What turned a banking crisis into a crisis of the state were the links between the bank and power. Peña was a shareholder in the controlling company until March 2025. Carlos Carvallo chaired Financiera UENO until July 2023 and then went on to head the Central Bank, the body that was supposed to supervise it. In addition, UENO managed some USD 850 million belonging to public bodies, including deposits of the Social Security Institute (IPS), which according to an investigation by the newspaper ABC Color had relaxed its internal rules between 2023 and 2024. For his part, Fernando Rivarola, the BCP's head of supervision who had warned about the bank's operations, was removed from his post. In this sense, the case is not about a supervisor who failed to see the problem, but about a structure in which oversight was inconvenient.

The financial response was swift. On 4 November Congress passed Law 7745, which brings forward payments from the Deposit Guarantee Fund of up to 40 minimum wages per depositor, about USD 19,800. By 9 November, 93.1% of customers had already received their guaranteed funds, and Moody's estimates that 97% of depositors will be fully covered. However, that figure hides another: according to the same rating agency, around 40% of deposits, excluding public ones, exceed the coverage. The fund protects most people, but not most of the money. Companies, pension funds and the state itself now depend on what is recovered in the liquidation.

The political response was even more abrupt. Peña resigned on 5 November, but Congress did not accept the resignation. Had the resignation been accepted, Peña would have become a senator for life with parliamentary immunity. Article 189 of the Constitution excludes from that office anyone who has been convicted in an impeachment trial. Deputies from his own Colorado Party voted for the indictment, former president Horacio Cartes withdrew his support, and on 6 November the Senate removed him for poor performance of his duties, with 44 votes in favour and one absence. In the words of the prosecuting deputy Rocío Vallejo, Peña “created a 1.2 billion dollar hole”, a figure that still has no official backing. Minutes later, the Public Prosecutor's Office ordered his arrest and that of Carvallo for breach of trust. The defence argues that the impeachment is void because the president had already resigned. That legal dispute is not settled.

It is remarkable that the financial system has held up better than the political class. In eight days the guaraní lost about 12% against the dollar and the spread on sovereign bonds rose by about 25 basis points. Even so, Moody's (Baa3) and S&P (BBB-) maintain investment grade, and the banking association Asoban says the other institutions are operating normally. Moody's warned, however, that the case reveals supervisory failures, corruption problems and doubts about the BCP's independence, and that this could weigh on the rating if the political crisis drags on. For a country that had just achieved its second investment-grade rating and presented itself as a safe destination for capital and new residents, the damage to its reputation may exceed the fiscal cost.

Alliana asked the entire cabinet to resign and proposed Rivarola for the BCP board. It is a signal of correction, but it has limits: several legislators mentioned him during the impeachment trial, and he will govern until 2028 with the same party under whose government the bank grew. Meanwhile, the investigation is already crossing borders. The bank was controlled through U Holdings, a company in Luxembourg in which Grupo Vázquez held 90% of the capital and 99% of the votes, and the Public Prosecutor's Office has requested cooperation from the United States, Luxembourg and Argentina to trace assets.

Whereas Argentina's 2001 corralito affected the whole system, the UENO case is the crisis of a single institution. Even so, it reveals something more general. It shows us that digital banking in Latin America has grown faster than the regulation of the conglomerates that control it. For the region, the question is not whether fintechs should exist, but who supervises them when their owners have direct access to political power. Paraguay has the opportunity to turn its biggest financial scandal into a governance reform. If it does not, the next crisis will have the same structure and a different brand.

Open questions

  • How much will the resolution cost the Paraguayan state once the assets are liquidated and it becomes clear what share of public and pension funds is not recovered?
  • Will Paraguay adopt rules that protect the independence of its Central Bank, such as cooling-off periods for executives coming from supervised institutions?
  • Does an impeachment resolved in two days strengthen accountability or set a precedent of institutional instability?
  • Will other Latin American regulators review how they supervise the conglomerates that control digital banks with millions of customers?
